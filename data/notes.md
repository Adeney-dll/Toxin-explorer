### Toxin-explorer notes

#### Terms to define

| **Descriptor**                | **What it means**                                                                     | **Biological Relevance for Toxins**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Molecular Weight (MW)**     | The physical size and mass of the molecule.                                           | **Diffusion:** Smaller molecules (under 500 g/mol) can easily squeeze between cells or slip through membranes. Large molecules often require specific transport proteins.                                                                                                         |
| **LogP (Lipophilicity)**      | A ratio of how well it dissolves in oil (fat) versus water.                           | **Membrane penetration:** A high LogP (> 3) means it dissolves easily in fats, allowing it to pass directly through cellular lipid membranes (like Batrachotoxin). A negative LogP means it is highly water-soluble and gets stuck outside the cell membrane (like Tetrodotoxin). |
| **H-Bond Donors (HBD)**       | The number of hydrogen atoms attached to Oxygen or Nitrogen that can "donate" a bond. | **Water solubility & binding:** High numbers make a molecule highly polar. Toxins with many H-bonds bind very tightly to water, making it extremely difficult for them to pass through fatty tissue like the skin or blood-brain barrier.                                         |
| **H-Bond Acceptors (HBA)**    | The number of Oxygen or Nitrogen atoms that can "accept" a hydrogen bond.             | Same as above. Together, HBD and HBA dictate how a toxin locks into its target receptor (like a key fitting into a specific lock).                                                                                                                                                |
| **Polar Surface Area (TPSA)** | The total surface area (in Å²) occupied by polar atoms (O, N, and their hydrogens).   | **Permeability:** A TPSA over 140 means the toxin absorbs poorly through cell walls. A TPSA under 90 is usually required for a toxin to cross the blood-brain barrier and attack the central nervous system.                                                                      |
| **Ring Count**                | The number of cyclical structures (rings) in the molecule.                            | **Rigidity:** Rings lock a molecule into a specific 3D shape. A rigid toxin often has a higher potency because it doesn't have to waste energy folding into the correct shape to plug a biological receptor.                                                                      |

### 1. The Route of Attack (Water vs. Fat)

The most important comparison you are making is how the toxins travel. Our bodies are mostly water (blood, cellular fluid) separated by barriers made of fat (cellular membranes, the blood-brain barrier).

- **LogP and TPSA** compare this directly.
    
- A toxin with a high LogP and low TPSA is a "ghost" that can pass effortlessly through fatty cellular walls and attack from the inside.
    
- A toxin with a negative LogP and high TPSA is trapped in the water. It cannot cross cell membranes easily, so it must attack targets on the _outside_ surface of cells.
    

### 2. The Locking Mechanism (The Key and the Lock)

Toxins work by plugging themselves into critical proteins (like nerve channels) and breaking them.

- **H-Bond Donors, H-Bond Acceptors, and Ring Count** compare how the toxin "docks" with its target.
    
- A high number of H-bonds means the toxin acts like molecular Velcro, clinging fiercely to the target protein.
    
- A high ring count means the toxin is rigid (like a metal key), allowing it to jam perfectly into a receptor without needing to fold or bend first.
    

### 3. The Physical Footprint

- **Molecular Weight** compares sheer bulk. A massive toxin might be too big to fit into certain cellular pores, while a tiny one might slip right through.
    

### The Real-World Result: Tetrodotoxin vs. Batrachotoxin

If you look at the numbers your script just generated, you are actually comparing two entirely different evolutionary strategies for attacking the exact same target: **the human nervous system (specifically, voltage-gated sodium channels).**

- **Tetrodotoxin (Pufferfish):** Has a high TPSA and negative LogP. It cannot cross the fatty cell membrane. Therefore, it swims through the blood and extracellular fluid, finds the sodium channel, and acts like a cork, **plugging it from the outside**. This stops nerves from firing, causing paralysis.
    
- **Batrachotoxin (Poison Dart Frog):** Has a high LogP. It loves fat. It easily dissolves directly into the fatty lipid membrane of the nerve cell. From inside the membrane, it binds to the sodium channel and **forces it locked open**. This causes nerves to fire uncontrollably, causing massive spasms and heart failure.
